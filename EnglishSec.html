EDITORIAL

Progress at What Cost?

by Rosario, Paul Aaron

Pax Silica may bring new investments, jobs, and technological development to the Philippines. However, we should not accept these benefits without considering the possible effects on our environment and communities. Large industries can require huge amounts of water, electricity, land, and natural resources, which could place additional pressure on resources that Filipinos already depend on.

The Philippines should not rush into Pax Silica simply because it promises economic growth. Before moving forward, the government should ensure that Filipino communities, workers, and the environment are properly protected. Development is important, but it should never come at the expense of the people and the resources that sustain our country.

Another important concern is the responsibility of both the government and participating companies to protect the environment. Industrial development must not come at the cost of polluted water, damaged ecosystems or communities losing access to the resources they need. Before approving major projects, the government should carefully evaluate their environmental impact and establish strict regulations to ensure that companies remain accountable for their actions. Economic growth should not become an excuse for neglecting environmental protection.

Moreover, we must ask who will benefit the most from these investments. While companies may create employment opportunities, this does not guarantee better living conditions for ordinary Filipinos. There is a risk that our country could become dependent on corporations for technological development instead of advancing its own industries and research. The Philippines should prioritize producing local talent, supporting Filipino businesses, and building a technological industry that serves our own national interests.

EDITORIAL

Our Land is not for Sale, Mr Presidents

by Chua, Louie Vigor

As world leaders arrive for the ASEAN Summit, thousands of Filipinos are taking to the streets of Manila with a clear message in their mind: U.S. President Donald Trump is not welcome here in our country. Behind the diplomatic meetings and speeches lies the real reason for their rage: the controversial Pax Silica project. What the U.S. calls a great business deal for technology and AI is actually a plan that threatens to take away local lands and sell out Filipino interests to foreign powers.

At the heart of these protests is a simple truth—no tech center or computer chip factory is worth the destruction of anyone's home. Building massive infrastructure for American interests threatens the farmland, communities, and livelihoods of ordinary Filipinos. Sacrificing local families for foreign technology deals is not real progress—it is foreign control taking over our land under the guise of modernization.

Donald Trump's presence at the summit is directly connected to pushing this deal forward. He is here to secure the Pax Silica project and tie the Philippines into an unfair agreement. By standing up against his visit, Filipinos are telling both Washington and our own government that our land, rights, and communities are not for sale. We must reject Pax Silica, stop the displacement of our people, and prove that the Philippines belongs to Filipinos.

New Job Opportunities…but at what price?

by Alnajes, Rebekah Ysabel

In this generation, AI or Artificial Intelligence, is expected to continue to shape our future lives. As countries compete to become a part of the now advancing technology industry, the Philippines is being presented with an opportunity to become part of the technology industry. Through a project named “Pax Silica”, where it hopes to be stood in New Clark City in Tarlac, as a supply chain and for the semiconductor and AI industries. It promises billions of dollars and thousands of business opportunities, it shows that the project is expecting a big step in their economic growth. For some, it means better careers and a strengthened economy. Though for people who have spent years living and working in agriculture, the future promised by using AI and semiconductors, may look different.

According to BCDA (Bases Conversion and Development Authority), the project could attract around US$40 billion to US$70 billion in investments and generate more than 130,000 jobs when fully developed. It shows a promising lifestyle, especially for Filipinos looking for better job opportunities. For many, it could mean having access to careers that were once available only abroad. Although, even though there are impressive technological plans, another story also unfolds among the communities surrounding New Clark City where the proposed project is expected to develop.

In Kapuso Mo, Jessica Soho’s documentary vlog, she visited parts of Capas, Tarlac, where the farmers and indigenous Filipinos raised their concerns about the proposed project. Even though the discussions about AI and semiconductors focus on innovation, the people interviewed in the vlog brought attention to something close to their home, their land, lifestyle, and what could possibly happen.

For them, the land is more than just their property, it is a part of their memory and lifestyle, it’s where they’ve lived, where their crops are grown, and where they get their own necessities. Farming isn’t just an occupation that can easily be replaced by tons of jobs, it’s a way of life passed down from generation to generation.

Some residents have also expressed their concerns about the lack of consultation or information regards to Pax Silica. Their questions: “Where would they go?”, “How would they continue to earn their living?” and “What would happen to their homes?” still remains unanswered.

A project can promise thousands of jobs, but those opportunities may not be enough for the people whose lives can be affected by this project. Pax Silica may bring economic growth, but it also requires massive land area, water, electricity and other resources that can affect our environment. These are the matters that deserves attention, especially when local communities depends on the same resources for their daily living.

Developing local semiconductor industries could create more career opportunities for Filipino engineers, researchers, and skilled workers. It could also help the country become more involved in the global technology industry instead of actually depending on imported products and expertise.

Progress shouldn’t only be measured by how much money a project can attract or how many jobs it can promise. The lives of the Filipinos living near its proposed location should also be part of the conversation.

As the plans for Pax Silica continue to develop by the government, other Filipinos deserve to have clear information, not just about its positive side, but also its possible consequences to people and the environment. Farmers and Indigenous people should also be given opportunities to express their concerns and understand the decisions that may affect their lives.

The future of technology may be built in New Clark City, but its story shouldn’t leave the people of Tarlac behind. Before celebrating what the Philippines might gain, it is significant to listen to those who are wondering what they might lose someday

A Silicon Future, A Safrificed Homeland

by Rosario, Paul Aaron

The Philippines has taken another step toward global technology industry through Pax Silica. Promising billions in investments, thousands of jobs, and advancements in artificial intelligence. The initiative appears to offer a brighter future. But behind these promises lies a question, is technological progress worth the possible sacrifice of Filipino communities and natural resources?

The Philippines should not rush into Pax Silica simply because it promises economic growth. Before moving forward, the government should ensure that Filipino communities, workers, and the environment are properly protected. Development is important, but it should never come at the expense of the people and the resources that sustain our country.

The possible displacement of Filipino farmers and Indigenous communities, whose livelihoods and ancestral lands may be threatened by industrial expansion. While the project promises employment opportunities, not everyone may possess the skills or qualifications required for these high-paying positions. Furthermore, the Philippines risks becoming dependent on foreign corporations, providing land, labor, and natural resources while much of the technological advancement and economic benefits remain in the hands of international investors. Without proper environmental assessments, transparency, and protection for affected communities, the promise of progress may come at a significant cost to ordinary Filipinos.

But, one of the biggest concerns is the project's environmental impact. The proposed industrial hub in New Clark City, Tarlac, is expected to require enormous amounts of electricity and water. Reports indicate that its projected water demand could reach 60 million liters daily, raising concerns about competition with agricultural and household needs.
